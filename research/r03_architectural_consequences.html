R–03

RECO / Reflective Companion

When Agent Architecture Becomes Interaction Design

The same conversational intentions produced different forms of reflection when organised as one prompt versus multiple functional nodes.

Period
Oct 2025 – Jan 2026
Type
LLM Architecture · Human–AI Interaction · SDT · Motivational Interviewing
Participants
20 participants · 11 paired participants · 22 conversations
Research question

Does the way conversational responsibilities are organised change what kind of support the agent actually provides?

01 / variable

Architecture is invisible to users—but its consequences are not

Two agents can share the same interface, model, purpose, safety boundaries, and design principles yet create different interactions.

Writing “be empathetic” does not guarantee an interaction will feel empathetic. Designing around Self-Determination Theory does not guarantee that people will experience autonomy, competence, or relatedness. Internal architecture therefore became a design variable rather than only an implementation detail.

02 / frameworks

SDT described the experience; MI described the behaviour

Two complementary frameworks connected psychological intent to observable interaction.

Self-Determination TheoryWhat experience do we want?

Autonomy · meaningful choice and ownership
Competence · feeling capable of understanding and moving forward
Relatedness · feeling respected, connected, and supported

Motivational InterviewingWhat conversational behaviour might support it?

Open questions · reflection · affirmation · collaboration · support for personal choice

SDT provided the experiential target; MI gave us a language for examining whether the agent’s dialogue actually enacted reflective and motivationally sensitive behaviour.

03 / architectures

Two ways of organising the same responsibilities

Single-promptIntegrated responsibility

Reflection, goal management, activity recommendation, safety guidance, tone, and interaction principles lived in one instruction space. The model could move flexibly between responsibilities without explicit state transitions.

Multi-nodeSeparated responsibility

Reflection, goal management, and activity recommendation were specialised into functional nodes connected through routing and intent detection, creating clearer boundaries and more explicit control.

The design intent was held as constant as possible. What changed was how those intentions were structurally organised.

04 / experience

Users did not simply rate one architecture as better

Task-level METUX ratings were broadly comparable across the two architectures.

Single-prompt

More open, emotionally attentive, and companion-like; offered space to explore but could feel directionless.

Multi-node

More structured, practical, and advisor-like; offered clearer progression but could move toward action before the person felt understood.

Similar need-support scores therefore concealed different interactional pathways.

05 / reflection

Reflection as process vs reflection as progress

Reflection as process

Stay with an experience → express it → interpret it → discover what matters.

Reflection as progress

Turn understanding into direction → clarify options → make decisions → identify a next step.

Neither form is inherently superior. The design problem is deciding when someone needs space to remain with an experience and when they need help moving forward.

06 / behaviour

MITI showed what the agent actually did

Behavioural annotation made the architectural difference visible in the dialogue itself.

Single-promptMore reflection

More simple reflections (p = .001), a higher total-reflection proportion (p = .005), and a higher reflection-to-question ratio (p = .009).

Multi-nodeMore information-giving

Higher information-giving proportion (p = .004). Zero-reflection cases occurred only in this condition: 3/11 and 4/11 conversations across the two annotators.

Prompt inspection tells us what the designer asked for. User ratings tell us what the person felt. Behavioural annotation adds a third layer: what the agent actually did.

Design takeaway

Agent architecture can support the same basic psychological needs through different interactional behaviours—and those differences may appear differently in user experience and observable agent behaviour.
