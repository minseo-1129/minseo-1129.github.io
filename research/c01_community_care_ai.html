C–01

Community Care AI

Mapping What Conversational AI Can Actually Do in Community Care

A capability-centred review moves the conversation from AI labels to concrete functions, responsibilities, and care-system implications.

Period
Jun 2025 – Mar 2026
Type
Scoping Review · Conversational AI · Community Care
Corpus
29 included studies
Research question

What capabilities have conversational AI systems actually demonstrated in community-care settings?

01 / framing

“AI assistant” is not a useful enough description

Labels such as “LLM chatbot”, “virtual assistant”, or “conversational agent” say surprisingly little about what a system actually contributes to a care pathway.

Community care is distributed across homes, neighbourhoods, frontline services, and professional teams. In that setting, practitioners need a vocabulary that describes concrete system functions rather than only underlying technology.

Model-centred question

Should this service use an LLM?

Capability-centred question

What should the system remember, assess, generate, or escalate—and who remains responsible?

02 / review

From 541 screened records to 29 studies

The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidance and searched biomedical and computing literature, supplemented with selected grey literature.

551records identified
541title / abstract screening after duplicates
65full-text assessment
29studies included

Capabilities were coded at the level of specific functional actions rather than treating each product or model as a single category. The final codebook was refined through independent coding, comparison, and consensus discussion.

03 / taxonomy

A 20-capability vocabulary

The resulting taxonomy organises 20 capabilities into five functional domains.

UnderstandIdentify · Detect · Classify · Rank · Assess
TransformTranscribe · Translate · Adapt
RememberStore · Retrieve · Recall
GenerateConverse · Answer · Summarise · Render · Write
ActNotify · Escalate · Schedule · Operate

The verbs are intentionally action-oriented. A team can reason about whether a system should recall past conversations or escalate a case more concretely than it can reason from a generic label such as “AI assistant”.

04 / role

Conversational AI often acted as infrastructure

Across the reviewed systems, conversational AI was commonly positioned as extending human care rather than replacing it.

AccessExtend reach

Answer questions, maintain contact, and support users outside conventional encounters.

CoordinationMove information

Store, summarise, notify, and connect information across people and services.

OversightKeep humans in the loop

Escalate uncertainty or risk and support frontline staff rather than assuming autonomous care.

Multi-party and expert-in-the-loop configurations were therefore recurring patterns. The value of conversational AI was often infrastructural: making care more reachable, continuous, and coordinated.

05 / responsibility

Every capability also creates a responsibility

A capability is not only a benefit. It changes what the surrounding care system must govern.

Reliability ↔ flexibilityOpen-ended generation may feel natural while becoming harder to govern in sensitive interactions.
Memory ↔ privacyContinuity can improve when systems remember, while privacy and governance risk increase with persistence.
Access ↔ exclusionScalable support can widen reach while creating barriers for users with severe symptoms, low digital literacy, or inaccessible interaction modes.
Automation ↔ labourMonitoring can scale, but someone still needs to interpret, follow up, and respond to what the system surfaces.

The taxonomy is therefore most useful when a capability is discussed together with the human responsibility, workflow, and safeguard that surrounds it.

Design takeaway

For conversational AI to be deployed responsibly in community care, we first need to understand what capabilities it actually provides—and match those capabilities to appropriate human roles and responsibilities.
