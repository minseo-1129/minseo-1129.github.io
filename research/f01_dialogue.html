F–01

ForMe · Dialogue Design

Designing ForMe’s Task-Based Dialogue Flow

Translating Goal–Plan–Do–Check into conversations that help children remember, perform, recover, and recognise success in everyday routines.

Period
Mar 2022 – Mar 2024
Type
Dialogue Design · Conversational Agent · Field Deployment
Participants
Children aged 6–7 with ADHD and mild / borderline intellectual functioning
Research question

How can a conversational agent scaffold everyday tasks without taking control of the task away from the child?

01 / principle

From self-instruction to conversation

ForMe was not designed simply to remind children what to do. The conversation itself needed to support remembering, planning, acting, and recognising successful completion.

I used Goal–Plan–Do–Check as the behavioural structure, then translated that structure into moments the conversational agent could actually support.

Behavioural structure
GoalRecall what needs to be done.
PlanPrepare how to begin.
DoPerform the task.
CheckConfirm completion.
02 / translation

A reusable dialogue grammar

I designed individual dialogue flows for routines such as reading, writing a diary, washing feet, changing clothes, preparing a water bottle, making the bed, and getting ready for sleep.

Goal–Plan–Do–Check provided the organising principle, but its conversational implementation was not a literal four-turn sequence.

From framework to interaction
Framework
Goal → Plan → Do → Check
Dialogue
Context → Recall → Repair → Plan / Prepare → Do → Check → Self-affirmation → Reward

The interaction often began with a contextual cue. Rather than immediately telling the child what to do, ForMe first gave them an opportunity to recall the task themselves.

03 / repair

Designing for breakdown

The useful interaction was not the ideal path. It was the system’s ability to keep the child moving when memory, motivation, or speech recognition broke down.

Instead of collapsing every failed response into the same fallback, the dialogue progressively changed the amount of support.

Repeat or rephrase the question.

Add a more explicit cue or hint.

Allow the child to say they do not know.

Provide the task only when more support is necessary.

Response states included correct and incorrect answers, no response, unrecognised speech, completed or incomplete tasks, refusal, and readiness.

04 / variation

One structure, different task depths

Reusability did not mean forcing every routine into an identical script. The amount of conversational structure changed with the task.

Simple routine

Washing feet

Recall the task → check whether it is already done → perform a timed action → confirm completion → recognise success.

Richer routine

Reading

Recall the task → choose the book → plan duration → perform the activity → reflect on emotion or learning → close with reinforcement.

05 / mastery

Closing the loop with mastery

Completion was deliberately made visible. After completing a routine, children were prompted to acknowledge what they had done through statements such as “I did it on my own.”

ForMe then reinforced completion with a star. The closing interaction therefore connected successful behaviour to the child’s own interpretation of competence.

Task completionI completed the routine.
Mastery experienceI recognise that I was able to do it.
06 / deployment

From dialogue flows to everyday use

The dialogue system was subsequently deployed in an eight-week home pilot.

8 / 10participants completed the study
8 weekshome deployment
76.1%average task compliance

The study also reported improvements in self-efficacy and ADHD-related behaviour. I treat these primarily as evidence that the interaction design could support repeated engagement with everyday routines over time, rather than attributing the psychological outcomes to a particular dialogue branch.

Small pilot study; psychological outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.

Design takeaway

Conversation design for supporting children’s executive function and self-regulation starts with understanding the full range of ways a child may respond—not only the ideal response.
