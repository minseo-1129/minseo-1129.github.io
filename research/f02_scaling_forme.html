F–02

ForMe

From a Child–Agent Interaction to a School–Family Support System

Scaling ForMe required designing not only for children, but for the people and institutions surrounding them.

Period
Aug 2022 – Aug 2024
Type
Conversational Agent · School Mental Health · Ecosystem Design
Participants
132 first-grade children · 20 schools
Research question

What should the surrounding support system look like when a child-facing conversational intervention is used at school scale?

01 / scale

The interaction could scale. Could the support system?

The first ForMe studies centred on a child interacting directly with a conversational agent. Moving the intervention into schools changed the design problem from a single interaction to a support ecosystem.

A child’s mental-health support is distributed across contexts. Parents observe everyday routines at home. Teachers see behaviour in school. Schools can widen access to early support, but also introduce questions of workload, stigma, responsibility, and information sharing.

ChildAct independently

Select, recall, and complete everyday tasks with the conversational agent.

ParentSee enough to support

Monitor task activity without turning the child-facing intervention into constant surveillance.

TeacherCoordinate at school

View participation and programme activity across students through a management interface.

Scaling ForMe therefore meant deciding not only what the child should experience, but what information other adults should receive—and what should remain with the child.

02 / system

Designing three connected interfaces

ForMe was expanded into three linked interfaces: a conversational-agent application for children, a monitoring application for parents, and a web interface for teachers.

ChildChoose and perform daily tasks
ParentMonitor task activity at home
TeacherOversee participation at school
SchoolProvide access and programme context

The interfaces were connected, but they did not need identical information. The design problem was to distribute visibility and responsibility according to each stakeholder’s role rather than collapsing everyone into one dashboard.

03 / deployment

Evaluating the system across 20 schools

The system was evaluated through a six-week intervention/control study across 20 elementary schools in collaboration with the Daegu Metropolitan Office of Education.

04 / perspective

One child, multiple perspectives

Parents and teachers did not necessarily describe the same child in the same way. At baseline in the intervention group, teacher ADHD-RS ratings were significantly higher than parent ratings across all three measures.

SystemDo not flatten difference

Multiple reports can be partial views of situated behaviour rather than a single authoritative truth.

The contrast is not simply noise to be averaged away. It shows why school mental-health systems need to preserve context: the same child can look different across home and school, and neither observer necessarily holds the whole picture.

05 / ecosystem

Scaling an intervention means redistributing care work

The school deployment made visible that scaling is not just a question of serving more users.

InformationWhat should move between child, parent, teacher, and school?
AutonomyHow much space should a child have to complete a task before adult intervention?
ResponsibilityWho is expected to notice, interpret, and act when the system surfaces a concern?
WorkloadWhat new monitoring and coordination work does a school-facing system create?

A system can make support more connected while simultaneously creating new responsibilities around interpretation, oversight, and follow-up.

Design takeaway

A system designed around a child’s wellbeing should reflect the different aspects of that child that become visible to different people across the care ecosystem.
